Wall and Drywall Water Damage: What Williamsburg Homeowners Should Know

Think of drywall as a wick. Paper and gypsum pull water up from the floor line, so the wet zone frequently ends a foot or more past the highest point the water itself touched. Insulation and the rear of the panel can be wet through while the paint out front shows nothing unusual. That is why we check walls with a moisture meter instead of trusting how they look.

Plenty of walls that get wet can stay. Firm panels wetted by clean water are frequently saved, in some cases with the baseboard taken off and small openings drilled so air can move inside the wall. If the drywall has gone soft, crumbles, shows mold or took in dirty water, we do a flood cut, meaning a level cut over the damaged area with all material beneath it removed.

What It Looks Like

  • Caulk lines cracking or gaps opening where trim meets the wall
  • Speckled growth showing up where furniture sits against the wall or at corners
  • Wall coverings lifting, blistering or creasing close to floor level
  • A wall surface that gives, feels mushy or breaks apart under your thumb
  • Cool, slightly clammy drywall next to walls at normal temperature
  • Mildew smell you notice most when standing close to one section of wall

Behind the Surface

The Damage You Do Not See

Walls You Cannot See

Wet drywall behind cabinets, vanities and built-ins gets no airflow and is easy to overlook. We take readings through toe kicks and from the opposite side to find it.

Water Next Door

Water passes under the bottom plate into the next room or closet. Every wall involved is tested on its two faces, and so is the adjacent flooring.

Insulation Holding Water

Once wet, fiberglass insulation stays wet and keeps the studs damp. No amount of airflow on the painted side will dry a cavity packed with wet batts.

Mold Inside Cavities

The back of the drywall is dark, still and lined with paper, which is ideal for mold. That side may be coated in growth even as the room side appears spotless.

Our Process

Our Wall and Drywall Water Damage Process

1

Map the Moisture

We scan each wall with a moisture meter and thermal camera and mark how high and how far the water traveled, including the opposite side and adjoining rooms.

2

Decide What Stays

Based on the water category, how long it sat and how the drywall feels, we tell you plainly which sections can be dried in place and which need to come out.

3

Cut or Ventilate

Walls we are keeping have their baseboards removed so air can be pushed into the cavity. Walls that cannot be kept get a flood cut along a level line, with the wet insulation pulled.

4

Drying and Reconstruction

Dehumidifiers and air movers stay on, usually 3 to 5 days, until the studs and the drywall left in place test dry. Then we insulate, hang and finish new drywall, reinstall trim and paint.

Why Speed Matters

How the Damage Progresses

First hours

Water Starts Climbing

Water at the base of the wall soaks into the drywall edge and starts to climb. Both the baseboard and the bottom plate start absorbing water.

24 to 48 hours

Wall Swells

Moisture keeps climbing, the baseboards puff up and blisters form in the paint. Growth is able to get going on the paper that lines the hidden side.

Day 3 to 7

Panels Lose Strength

Paper peels from a core that is no longer firm. Within the wall the insulation sags, a smell develops and mold moves over the back of the panel.

After a week

Damage Reaches the Studs

Mold and the start of decay show up on framing that has been wet the whole time. The amount of removal and reconstruction grows considerably.
